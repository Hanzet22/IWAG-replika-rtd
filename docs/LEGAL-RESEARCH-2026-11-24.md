# Global legal register research — Parallel

As-of: 24 November 2026

## Global legal register — status at 24 November 2026

### 1. Scope and operating assumptions

This register assumes an independently operated Indonesian convention-information website that is presently read-only but may later add accounts, profiles, comments, private messages, event registration, ticketing, vendors, advertising or other community functions. Mere worldwide accessibility normally does **not** trigger every foreign law. The usual triggers are establishment, intentionally offering goods or services, monitoring people, processing residents’ data, local commercial activity, or operating a regulated intermediary/social-media service.

Use three applicability tiers:

- **Now:** privacy, cookies/analytics, security, consumer-law and accessibility rules can apply to an informational site when it collects personal data, targets residents or sells event services.
- **Feature-triggered:** children’s rules, direct-marketing rules, intermediary safe harbours and platform-safety duties become material when accounts, user-generated content, messaging, targeted advertising or ticketing are introduced.
- **Platform-only:** laws expressly confined to social networks, user-to-user services, app-distribution services or regulated intermediaries ordinarily do not govern a genuinely static publisher merely because its pages can be read locally.

Official translations identified as unofficial or reference-only are not themselves binding; the original-language statute controls. Regulator codes and guidance may be admissible or influential but are not statutes unless expressly incorporated into a statutory regime.

## 2. Indonesia: home-law baseline

- **Personal Data Protection Law — Law No. 27 of 2022:** binding and fully effective after its transition period. Relevant provisions include Articles 2–3 (scope), 20 (processing bases), 21–22 (consent), 25 (children), 35–39 (controller duties), 46 (breach notice), 48 (corporate changes) and 56 (international transfers). Official legislation portal: https://peraturan.go.id/id/uu-no-27-tahun-2022.
- **Electronic Information and Transactions Law — Law No. 11 of 2008, amended by Laws No. 19 of 2016 and No. 1 of 2024; Government Regulation No. 71 of 2019:** binding rules for electronic-system operators, electronic information and unlawful content. Registration and governance obligations depend on whether the operation is a covered private electronic-system operator, not merely on owning informational HTML pages.
- **Government Regulation No. 17 of 2025 on Governance of Electronic Systems in Child Protection:** binding child-safety framework; potentially relevant if the site is likely to be accessed by children or adds accounts/community features. Official text: https://peraturan.go.id/files/pp-no-17-tahun-2025.pdf.
- **Consumer Protection Law No. 8 of 1999** and **Trade Through Electronic Systems Regulation, Government Regulation No. 80 of 2019:** become important for paid tickets, merchandise, sponsorship packages or vendor transactions. Key consumer-law provisions include Articles 4, 7, 8 and 18 of Law No. 8/1999.
- **Persons with Disabilities Law No. 8 of 2016:** establishes accessibility/equality obligations, but it does not create a universal WCAG conformance rule for every private informational website. WCAG 2.2 AA is nevertheless the prudent technical baseline.

## 3. European Union and EEA

### GDPR

- **Instrument/status:** Regulation (EU) 2016/679, directly applicable and in force. Official text: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj.
- **Territorial trigger:** Article 3 covers an EU/EEA establishment and a non-EU operator that intentionally offers goods or services to people in the Union or monitors their behaviour there. Accessibility alone is insufficient; EU languages, currencies, shipping/ticket sales, local advertising or behavioural tracking are relevant evidence.
- **Operational pinpoints:** Articles 5–6 (principles and legal bases), 7 (consent), 8 (child consent for information-society services), 9 (special-category data), 12–14 (notices), 15–22 (rights), 25 (privacy by design/default), 26–28 (joint controllers/processors), 30 (records), 32–34 (security and breach), 35 (DPIA), 37–39 (DPO) and 44–49 (international transfers). An Article 27 representative may be required for a targeted non-EU controller, subject to Article 27(2).
- **Website effect:** applies to personal-data processing such as analytics identifiers, forms, mailing lists and event accounts; it is not dependent on having social features.

### Digital Services Act

- **Instrument/status:** Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, fully applicable since 17 February 2024. Official text: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/2065/oj.
- **Scope:** Articles 2–3 cover intermediary services offered to recipients located or established in the Union, including mere conduit, caching and hosting. A publisher of its own convention pages is not thereby a hosting service or online platform.
- **Feature-triggered provisions:** Articles 11–13 (contact points and, where applicable, EU legal representative), 14 (terms), 16–17 (notice/action and reasons), 20–21 (complaints and dispute settlement), 22 (trusted flaggers), 23 (misuse), 24 (transparency), 25 (dark patterns), 26 (advertising) and 28 (minors). Articles 16–28 principally matter when the service stores and disseminates users’ content; Article 28 is for online platforms accessible to minors. Commission/EDPB explanatory material is guidance, not a substitute for the Regulation.

## 4. United Kingdom

- **UK GDPR:** retained and amended Regulation (EU) 2016/679, binding. Use Articles 3, 5–9, 12–22, 25, 28, 32–35 and 44–49. The UK child-consent threshold is 13 under Article 8. Official text: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679.
- **Data Protection Act 2018, 2018 c.12:** binding and supplements the UK GDPR, including special-category/criminal-data conditions in Schedules 1–2, enforcement and remedies, and the statutory age-appropriate-design mandate in section 123. Official text: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2018/12/contents.
- **Children’s Code/Age Appropriate Design Code:** an ICO statutory code under DPA 2018 section 123, containing 15 standards. It can cover news and information services likely to be accessed by under-18s, even where children are not the intended audience. It is not a separate Act, but compliance is relevant to demonstrating observance of data-protection law. Official code: https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/.
- **Online Safety Act 2023, 2023 c.50:** binding, with duties brought into force in stages and Ofcom codes operating by the stated date. Sections 3–4 define regulated user-to-user and search services and UK links; sections 9–12 contain illegal-content and child-safety duties; sections 22–31 address reporting/complaints and related duties. A read-only publisher with no user-to-user or search functionality is generally outside those regulated-service categories. If comments, forums, profiles or messaging are added, reassess immediately. Official text: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/contents. Providers of regulated user-to-user services likely to be accessed by children must perform the section 11 assessment and comply with section 12 duties.
- **Accessibility/consumer:** Equality Act 2010 sections 20 and 29 may require reasonable adjustments by service providers. Consumer Rights Act 2015 and Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations apply if tickets or services are sold; they are not triggered merely by publishing neutral information.

## 5. United States

Federal and state laws are threshold- and activity-specific; there is no single comprehensive federal website-privacy statute.

- **COPPA:** Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act, 15 U.S.C. §§6501–6506, and FTC Rule, 16 C.F.R. Part 312, binding. It applies to child-directed websites/online services and general-audience operators with actual knowledge that they collect personal information online from a child under 13. Pinpoints: 16 C.F.R. §§312.2–312.6, especially notice, verifiable parental consent, parental rights, minimisation/retention and security. Official source: https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule-coppa. A convention site is not automatically child-directed; subject matter, visuals, audience evidence and actual knowledge matter.
- **DMCA safe harbour:** Digital Millennium Copyright Act, 17 U.S.C. §512. Hosting user content may engage §512(c); eligibility requires, among other things, a registered agent, compliant notice-and-takedown process and a reasonably implemented repeat-infringer policy under §512(i). A site publishing only its own material needs ordinary copyright compliance, not §512(c) protection. Official source: https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap5.html#512.
- **ADA:** Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990, particularly Title III, 42 U.S.C. §§12181–12189. Whether a stand-alone website is a “place of public accommodation” varies among federal circuits; do not state universal coverage. A website sufficiently connected with a covered event venue or ticketing service presents greater exposure. DOJ material supports accessible web content but does not establish one universal private-sector WCAG mandate: https://www.ada.gov/resources/web-guidance/.
- **FTC Act:** 15 U.S.C. §45 prohibits unfair or deceptive practices. A misleading privacy promise, unsafe data practice or deceptive ticket sale can be actionable regardless of whether a comprehensive state privacy statute applies.
- **State privacy laws:** test users, revenue, data-volume, sale/targeted-advertising and small-business thresholds separately. Binding regimes in force by the stated date include California CCPA/CPRA (Cal. Civ. Code §§1798.100–1798.199.100), Virginia VCDPA (§§59.1-575–59.1-585), Colorado CPA (Colo. Rev. Stat. §§6-1-1301 et seq.), Connecticut DPA (Conn. Gen. Stat. §§42-515–42-526), Utah UCPA (§§13-61-101 et seq.), Texas TDPSA (Bus. & Com. Code ch. 541), Oregon CPA (ORS §§646A.570 et seq.), Montana MCDPA, Delaware PDPA, Iowa CDPA, Indiana CDPA, Tennessee TIPA, Florida Digital Bill of Rights, New Jersey DPA, New Hampshire DPA, Nebraska DPA, Minnesota Consumer Data Privacy Act, Maryland Online Data Privacy Act, Kentucky Consumer Data Protection Act and Rhode Island Data Transparency and Privacy Protection Act. Several exempt small entities or require substantial resident/data/revenue thresholds; none should be labelled universally applicable to this site without the relevant facts.
- **California-specific overlays:** the CCPA’s notice/rights provisions begin at §§1798.100, 1798.105, 1798.110, 1798.120 and 1798.130; “sale” and “sharing” require opt-out mechanisms. CalOPPA, Bus. & Prof. Code §§22575–22579, separately requires posted privacy disclosures from covered commercial websites collecting California residents’ personally identifiable information. State unfair/deceptive-practices, automatic-renewal, ticketing and biometrics laws must also be checked feature by feature.

## 6. Canada

- **PIPEDA:** Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act, S.C. 2000, c.5, binding for covered commercial activities and interprovincial/international flows. Pinpoints: sections 4–5; Schedule 1 principles 4.1–4.10; sections 10.1–10.3 for breach reporting/records. Cross-border processors remain subject to the transferring organisation’s accountability under principle 4.1.3. Official text: https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/P-8.6/. Purely non-commercial activity may fall outside PIPEDA; ticketing, sponsorship and paid memberships are stronger triggers.
- **Provincial substitution:** Alberta, British Columbia and Quebec have substantially similar private-sector laws for specified intraprovincial processing, but PIPEDA can still govern interprovincial/international transactions.
- **Quebec Law 25:** the binding underlying statute is the **Act respecting the protection of personal information in the private sector**, CQLR c. P-39.1, as modernised by **2021, c.25**. All principal reform stages are effective. Pinpoints include sections 3.1 (privacy officer/governance), 3.3 (policies), 3.5 (confidentiality incident), 3.8 (privacy by default), 4 and 8–8.1 (collection/transparency), 12–14 (consent, including minors), 17 (outside-Quebec assessment) and 18.3 (service providers). Official current text: https://legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca/en/document/cs/P-39.1. Application requires a sufficient Quebec enterprise/activity nexus; a globally visible page alone is not conclusive.
- **Accessibility/consumer:** federal and provincial human-rights/accessibility laws vary. Paid events must also be tested under provincial consumer-protection and ticketing rules.

## 7. Australia and New Zealand

### Australia

- **Privacy Act 1988, No. 119 of 1988:** binding; current compilation includes later amendments. Sections 6C–6D and 13 define covered acts/entities; section 5B addresses extraterritorial operation; Schedule 1 contains the Australian Privacy Principles, including APPs 1, 3, 5, 6, 8, 11, 12 and 13; Part IIIC contains the notifiable-data-breach scheme. Official current text: https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A03712/latest/text. Small-business and journalism exemptions must be assessed rather than assumed.
- **Online Safety Act 2021, No. 76 of 2021:** binding, as amended. It regulates specified online service providers and harmful material, not every read-only page in the same way. Relevant provisions include Part 4A for the social-media minimum age and Parts 6–8 for removal/remedial schemes. Official text: https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2021A00076/latest/text.
- **Age assurance:** Part 4A was inserted in 2024 and applies to age-restricted social-media platforms for Australian-resident account holders; subsequent **Online Safety Amendment (Strengthening Enforcement for the Social Media Minimum Age) Act 2026, No. 83 of 2026** strengthened the regime. It does not convert every informational convention site into an age-restricted social-media platform. OAIC/eSafety guidance is nonbinding explanatory material unless reflected in the statute, rules or enforceable standards.
- **Accessibility/consumer:** Disability Discrimination Act 1992, especially sections 5–6 and 24, can affect online services. Australian Consumer Law, Competition and Consumer Act 2010 Schedule 2, sections 18, 29 and 54–62, becomes material for ticketing and other paid services.

### New Zealand

- **Privacy Act 2020, Public Act 2020 No. 31:** binding and in force. Section 4 supplies extraterritorial application; section 22 contains IPPs 1–13; IPP 12 governs overseas disclosure; sections 112–122 govern notifiable privacy breaches. Official text: https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2020/0031/latest/whole.html. The Act applies to covered agencies processing personal information, not only commercial operators.
- The **Online Safety (Minimum Age and Child Safety Risk Assessment) Bill 2026** was a bill, not an enacted Act on the stated date; it must not be presented as binding law.
- Human Rights Act 1993 and Fair Trading Act 1986 may become relevant to accessibility and commerce, but neither creates a universal rule that every foreign static page is subject merely because it is visible in New Zealand.

## 8. Singapore, Malaysia and Thailand

### Singapore

- **Personal Data Protection Act 2012, No. 26 of 2012:** binding. Key provisions: sections 2–4 (scope/exclusions), 13–17 (consent), 18–20 (purpose/notification/access), 24–26 (accuracy, protection, retention and transfer limitation), 26A–26E (breaches), 26H–26M (data portability when commenced/applicable) and 48H–48J (accountability/enforcement). Official text: https://sso.agc.gov.sg/Act/PDPA2012. The transfer-limitation obligation is supplemented by the PDPA Regulations.
- **Online Safety (Relief and Accountability) Act 2025, No. 23 of 2025:** binding and commenced with the Online Safety Commission on 29 June 2026. It creates remedies, directions, duties and torts concerning specified online harmful activities and online services. Official text: https://sso.agc.gov.sg/Act/OSRAA2025. Coverage must be tested against the statutory service/activity definitions; a passive publisher is not automatically equivalent to a social-media or user-hosting service.
- Singapore’s accessibility guidance is generally nonbinding unless incorporated into a contract, sector rule or public-sector requirement. Consumer Protection (Fair Trading) Act 2003 and Electronic Transactions Act 2010 become relevant to transactions.

### Malaysia

- **Personal Data Protection Act 2010, Act 709**, amended by the **Personal Data Protection (Amendment) Act 2024, Act A1727:** binding for personal data processed in commercial transactions, subject to section 2 and the federal/state-government exclusion in section 3. Pinpoints include sections 5–12 (principles), 40 (sensitive data), 43–44 (rights), 129 (transfers), plus amendment provisions introducing controller terminology, processor security responsibility, DPO, breach notification and portability. Official texts: https://lom.agc.gov.my/act-view.php?type=pub&lang=BI&no=709 and https://www.pdp.gov.my/ppdpv1/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Act-A1727.pdf. The 2024 amendments commenced in phases; implementation should use the applicable commencement orders and current Commissioner circulars rather than assume every new duty began on Royal Assent.
- **Online Safety Act 2025, Act 866:** binding and in force under its commencement arrangements. It regulates designated licensed application/content service providers and prescribed services. Official text: https://lom.agc.gov.my/ilims/upload/portal/akta/outputaktap/2867049_BI/Act%20866-Online%20Safety%20Act%202025.pdf. It is not a universal code for every static foreign webpage; community/social functions and Malaysian communications licensing status are decisive.
- Consumer Protection Act 1999, especially sections 10–18 and Part IIIA, becomes material for online ticket/service sales. Malaysia lacks a general private-sector statutory WCAG rule applying to every website.

### Thailand

- **Personal Data Protection Act B.E. 2562 (2019):** binding, with its principal obligations effective since 2022. Section 5 covers Thai establishment and non-Thai offering/monitoring triggers; sections 19–20 address consent and minors; sections 23–26 notices and collection; sections 28–29 overseas transfers; sections 30–36 rights; and section 37 controller security/breach duties. Official English reference translation: https://www.mdes.go.th/uploads/tinymce/source/สคส/Personal%20Data%20Protection%20Act%202019.pdf. The Thai original controls.
- Direct marketing, cookies and event registration are privacy/consumer issues; Thailand’s PDPA is not an intermediary-content statute. Consumer Protection Act B.E. 2522 and direct-sales/direct-marketing legislation should be tested if commerce is launched.

## 9. Japan, South Korea, Philippines and India

### Japan

- **Act on the Protection of Personal Information, Act No. 57 of 2003 (APPI), as amended:** binding. Relevant provisions include Articles 16–23 (purpose, appropriate acquisition, security, breach and transparency), 27–30 (third-party and foreign transfers) and 171 (specified extraterritorial application/enforcement). Official PPC reference translation: https://www.ppc.go.jp/files/pdf/APPI_english.pdf; Japanese text controls.
- APPI has no single statutory child-consent age equivalent to GDPR Article 8; capacity and heightened transparency must be assessed contextually. A foreign operator is not covered solely because Japanese users can reach it; obtaining data in connection with supplying goods/services to persons in Japan is the important trigger.

### South Korea

- **Personal Information Protection Act, Act No. 10465 of 2011, as amended, including the version effective 11 September 2026:** binding Korean text. Pinpoints include Articles 3, 15–18 (principles/bases), 20 (notice), 22 and 22-2 (consent/children), 28-8 and 28-9 (cross-border transfers and suspension), 29 (security), 34 (breach) and 30 (privacy policy). Official database: https://www.law.go.kr/LSW/eng/engLsInfoR.do?lsiSeq=283839. The database warns that its English translation has no official legal effect.
- Do not infer universal jurisdiction from website availability; Korean nexus, data-subject targeting and applicable statutory scope require fact-specific analysis.

### Philippines

- **Data Privacy Act of 2012, Republic Act No. 10173:** binding. Sections 4 and 6 address scope/extraterritorial links; sections 11–13 contain principles and lawful criteria; section 16 rights; sections 20–21 security/accountability; section 25 addresses unauthorised processing. Official text: https://privacy.gov.ph/data-privacy-act/.
- The implementing rules elaborate notice, security, breach and outsourcing. The Act may extend to processing abroad where the data concerns Philippine citizens/residents and the entity has the statutory Philippine link; citizenship alone should not be treated as an unlimited worldwide trigger.

### India

- **Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023, Act No. 22 of 2023**, and **Digital Personal Data Protection Rules 2025:** binding, but commenced in statutory phases rather than all at once. Official sources: https://www.indiacode.nic.in/handle/123456789/22087 and https://indiacode.gov.in/items/fe07b131-481d-4cc9-bdb5-7bd218ced646.
- Pinpoints: Act sections 3 (scope, including offering goods/services to data principals in India), 4–7 (grounds/notice/consent/legitimate uses), 8 (fiduciary duties), 9 (children), 10 (significant fiduciaries), 11–13 (rights), 16 (government transfer restrictions) and 17 (exemptions). Rules cover notices, consent managers, security, breach, children, significant fiduciaries and rights.
- As of 24 November 2026, consult Rule 1’s commencement timetable provision by provision: administrative/Board provisions began first, additional provisions began after one year, and the remaining operational provisions were assigned the longer transition. Do not describe the entire Act and every Rule as simultaneously operational. A static page with no collection has little DPDP exposure; registration, analytics and ticketing change that result.

## 10. Brazil and Africa

### Brazil

- **Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados Pessoais, Law No. 13,709 of 2018 (LGPD):** binding. Articles 3–4 govern scope/exclusions; Articles 6–11 principles and legal bases; Article 14 children; Articles 18–22 rights; Articles 33–36 transfers; and Articles 46–49 security/breach. Official ANPD English reference: https://www.gov.br/anpd/pt-br/centrais-de-conteudo/outros-documentos-e-publicacoes-institucionais/lgpd-en-lei-no-13-709-capa.pdf.
- Article 3 can cover processing in Brazil, data collected in Brazil or processing aimed at offering goods/services to individuals located there. Visibility alone is not the same as offering services.
- Consumer Defense Code, Law No. 8,078/1990, applies to covered consumer transactions. Brazilian Inclusion Law No. 13,146/2015, Article 63, expressly addresses accessibility of websites maintained by companies headquartered or commercially represented in Brazil; do not automatically extend that territorial formulation to every foreign informational site.

### South Africa

- **Protection of Personal Information Act 4 of 2013 (POPIA):** binding, principal provisions operational since 1 July 2021. Sections 3–4 concern application; sections 8–25 contain the processing conditions; sections 34–35 regulate children’s information; section 55 the information officer; section 72 cross-border transfers; and sections 22–23 security-compromise notification. Official text: https://www.gov.za/documents/protection-personal-information-act.
- POPIA generally requires processing by a responsible party domiciled in South Africa or using means there, other than mere transit. A passive foreign page is not automatically covered.
- Electronic Communications and Transactions Act 25 of 2002, Chapter VII, applies to covered consumer e-commerce; Promotion of Equality and Prevention of Unfair Discrimination Act 4 of 2000 may be relevant to accessibility but does not specify universal WCAG conformance.

### Nigeria

- **Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023:** binding. Sections 2–3 govern application/exemptions; sections 24–25 principles/bases; section 31 children; sections 40–41 transfers; sections 34–35 rights; and section 40’s surrounding provisions and Commission instruments address international transfers. Official Gazette text: https://ndpc.gov.ng/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/Nigeria_Data_Protection_Act_2023.pdf.
- Territorial application requires the Act’s Nigerian establishment/residence or targeting/processing nexus; do not equate global reachability with Nigerian establishment.
- Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Act 2018 applies to covered commerce. Accessibility obligations may arise under the Discrimination Against Persons with Disabilities (Prohibition) Act 2018, but no universal private-site WCAG rule should be asserted without a relevant nexus.

### Kenya

- **Data Protection Act 2019, No. 24 of 2019:** binding. Sections 4 and 25–29 cover application, principles and lawful processing; section 33 concerns children; sections 41–42 address transfers; sections 43–47 security/breach and DPIAs. Official text: https://www.odpc.go.ke/dpa-act/.
- The Act includes processing connected with data subjects located in Kenya by controllers/processors established there or, under its extraterritorial wording, processing Kenyan residents’ data in the statutory circumstances. A factual targeting/nexus analysis remains necessary.
- Consumer Protection Act 2012 and Persons with Disabilities Act 2003 may apply to commerce/services; neither justifies claiming a universally binding WCAG level for every foreign informational page.

## 11. Middle East

### United Arab Emirates

- **Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 Concerning the Protection of Personal Data:** binding and effective. Articles 2–4 address scope/exclusions; Articles 5–6 principles/consent; Articles 13–18 rights; Articles 20–21 security and breach; and Articles 22–23 cross-border transfers. Official text: https://uaelegislation.gov.ae/en/legislations/1972/download?download=true. It covers specified UAE personal-data processing even where a controller or processor is outside the UAE, subject to statutory scope and free-zone exclusions.
- **Federal Decree-Law No. 26 of 2025 Regarding Child Digital Safety:** binding national child-digital framework assigning responsibilities to digital platforms, providers, parents and public bodies. **Cabinet Resolution No. 106 of 2026 Regarding Regulating Children’s Access to Social Media Platforms** is active from 30 June 2026 and applies to social-media platforms made available in or directed at UAE users. Official overview: https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/social-affairs/children/Childrens-digital-safety; resolution page: https://uaelegislation.gov.ae/en/legislations/4506.
- The social-media resolution is platform-specific and should not be applied to a static convention publication. Reassess if user accounts, feeds, interactions or messaging are introduced.

### Saudi Arabia

- **Personal Data Protection Law**, issued by Royal Decree No. M/19 of 9/2/1443H and amended by Royal Decree No. M/148 of 5/9/1444H, with its Implementing Regulations: binding. Pinpoints include Articles 2 (scope), 5–6 (consent/bases), 12–13 (notice), 18 (disclosure), 19–20 (security/breach), 25 (direct marketing) and 29 (transfers). Official source: https://dgp.sdaia.gov.sa/wps/portal/pdp/knowledgecenter/details/PDPL/.
- The Implementing Regulation’s Article 11 requires consent to be freely given, purpose-specific and documented; direct marketing requires identifiable senders, evidence of consent and a free/easy opt-out, with sending stopped upon withdrawal. Those are binding regulations, not optional marketing guidance.
- Extraterritorial coverage includes processing personal data relating to individuals residing in the Kingdom by an entity outside it, subject to statutory detail. Static publication alone is not processing, but analytics, subscriptions and event forms are.

## 12. China, Taiwan and Hong Kong

### China

- **Personal Information Protection Law of the PRC, adopted 20 August 2021 and effective 1 November 2021:** binding Chinese law. Articles 3 and 53 contain extraterritorial scope/representative requirements; Articles 5–9 principles; Articles 13–17 bases/notices; Articles 28–32 sensitive information and minors under 14; Articles 38–43 cross-border transfers; and Articles 51–57 security, DPIA and breach duties. Official Chinese source: http://www.npc.gov.cn/npc/c30834/202108/a8c4e3672c74491a80b53a172bb753fe.shtml.
- Article 3 reaches overseas processing intended to provide products/services to people in China or analyse/evaluate their behaviour. Mere passive availability should not be treated as conclusive targeting.
- PRC e-commerce and accessibility overlays depend on whether the service conducts e-commerce or falls under domestic service/accessibility rules.

### Taiwan

- **Personal Data Protection Act**, most recently amended 11 November 2025; binding provisions depend on their individually prescribed commencement dates. Official text/status: https://law.moj.gov.tw/ENG/LawClass/LawAll.aspx?pcode=I0050021.
- Pinpoints: Articles 2, 5, 8–9 (definitions/principles/notices), 19–20 (non-government collection/use), 21 (international-transfer restrictions), 27 (security) and 51 (exclusions and potential application to foreign entities). Do not treat uncommenced 2025 amendments as operative merely because enacted.
- The PDPA contains no broad GDPR-style child-consent article. Consumer Protection Act and disability/accessibility measures require a separate transactional/local-nexus analysis.

### Hong Kong

- **Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance, Cap. 486:** binding. Schedule 1 Data Protection Principles 1–6 cover collection, accuracy/retention, use, security, openness and access; Part VIA regulates direct marketing; sections 26 and 33 concern erasure and cross-border transfer respectively. Official text: https://www.elegislation.gov.hk/hk/cap486.
- Section 33 had still not been brought into force on the stated date, although other PDPO provisions and contractual accountability can regulate processing after transfer.
- The PDPO generally lacks broad extraterritorial effect and ordinarily requires a data user controlling operations in or from Hong Kong. Anti-doxxing cessation powers are a specific exception and do not create universal jurisdiction over ordinary foreign informational pages.

## 13. Türkiye and Switzerland

### Türkiye

- **Law No. 6698 on the Protection of Personal Data (KVKK), published 7 April 2016, as amended:** binding. Articles 2–4 cover scope/principles; Articles 5–6 processing; Articles 8–9 domestic and international transfers; Articles 10–12 notice, rights and security; Articles 16 and 18 registration/penalties. Official English reference: https://www.kvkk.gov.tr/Icerik/6649/Personal-Data-Protection-Law; Turkish text controls.
- Law No. 6698 does not state a GDPR-style general extraterritorial test. Establishment, processing location, targeting and conflict-of-laws principles therefore require careful analysis; do not infer jurisdiction solely from Turkish visitors.
- Law No. 6563 on Electronic Commerce regulates commercial electronic communications and e-commerce. Accessibility obligations require separate disability/equality analysis.

### Switzerland

- **Federal Act on Data Protection of 25 September 2020, SR 235.1 (revised FADP):** binding since 1 September 2023. Articles 3 (effects in Switzerland), 6–8 (principles/security), 16–17 (foreign disclosure), 19–21 (information), 22 (DPIA), 24 (breach) and 25–27 (rights) are central. Official text: https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/en.
- Article 3 is an effects test: conduct initiated abroad can be covered when it has effects in Switzerland. That does not mean every accessible website necessarily creates the required effect.
- The FADP has no GDPR-equivalent fixed digital-consent age. The Disability Discrimination Act primarily targets public services and specified offerings; avoid claiming universal private-website WCAG coverage.

## 14. Mexico, Argentina and Chile

### Mexico

- **Federal Law for the Protection of Personal Data Held by Private Parties**, new law published 20 March 2025 and last amended 14 November 2025: binding current text. Official source: https://www.diputados.gob.mx/LeyesBiblio/pdf/LFPDPPP.pdf. It is supplemented by the existing implementing regulation where compatible.
- Use the current consolidated text for exact numbering; core topics include principles and consent, privacy notices, ARCO rights, security/breach and domestic/international transfers. The implementing regulation’s Article 4 supplies territorial rules, including a Mexican establishment and processing by a processor, wherever located, on behalf of a controller established in Mexico. Do not transplant article numbers from the repealed 2010 statute without checking the 2025 consolidation.
- Consumer Protection Federal Law applies to covered event/ticket sales; Mexican accessibility obligations do not support an unqualified universal-WCAG claim for every foreign informational website.

### Argentina

- **Personal Data Protection Law No. 25,326 of 2000** and Regulatory Decree No. 1558/2001: binding. Articles 1, 4–6 cover scope/principles/notice/consent; Articles 11–12 disclosure and international transfers; Articles 14–16 rights; Article 25 processors. Official text: https://www.argentina.gob.ar/normativa/nacional/64790/actualizacion.
- The law centres on databases/processing with an Argentine legal or territorial nexus. Do not assert GDPR-style worldwide application without such a nexus. Consumer Protection Law No. 24,240 becomes relevant to paid event services.

### Chile

- **Current on 24 November 2026:** Law No. 19,628 remained the operative personal-data statute. **Law No. 21,719**, published 13 December 2024, had deferred commencement on **1 December 2026** and was therefore enacted but not yet effective on the register date. Official source/status: https://www.bcn.cl/leychile/navegar?idNorma=1209272.
- Once effective, Law No. 21,719 adds Article 1 bis territorial triggers, including controllers/processors established in Chile and specified foreign targeting/monitoring circumstances, together with modernised rights, duties, sanctions and a regulator. Until 1 December, do not present those new duties as already operative.
- Consumer Protection Law No. 19,496 applies to covered ticketing/services. Accessibility must be assessed under disability/equality law and the facts, not presumed from privacy legislation.

## 15. Practical feature gates and controls

1. **Static informational launch:** publish controller identity/contact details; use a jurisdiction-aware privacy/cookie notice; minimise analytics; execute processor agreements; secure logs and forms; create retention/deletion schedules; provide accessible navigation, headings, captions, contrast and keyboard support.
2. **Newsletter or contact forms:** map consent or another legal basis, record source/time, support withdrawal, screen direct-marketing laws, and implement breach response and cross-border-transfer mechanisms.
3. **Event registration/ticketing:** add consumer terms, total-price/refund disclosures, payment-security controls, tax/merchant analysis and accommodation procedures. Collect health/accessibility information only when necessary and with an appropriate sensitive-data basis.
4. **Accounts/community content:** before release, perform child-access and intermediary-law assessments; install reporting, moderation, appeals, recordkeeping, repeat-infringer and law-enforcement request processes; reassess DSA, UK OSA, Australian OSA, Singapore OSRAA, Malaysia Act 866, UAE child law and DMCA §512.
5. **Children:** avoid behavioural advertising and unnecessary profiling; use age-appropriate notices and high-privacy defaults. Do not deploy intrusive age verification merely because one platform-only regime exists—use a proportionate, jurisdiction- and feature-specific assessment.
6. **Transfers:** inventory hosting, email, analytics, payment and moderation vendors. Use the applicable transfer mechanism—such as EU SCCs/adequacy, UK IDTA/Addendum, or jurisdiction-specific adequacy/consent/contract rules—and document transfer risk where required.
7. **Accessibility:** WCAG 2.2 AA is a defensible engineering baseline, not a statement that one identical legal standard binds the site in every country.

This is a legal-register and issue-spotting document, not a conclusion that all listed instruments presently apply. Before enabling community, child-directed, ticketing or vendor functions, obtain local advice for the countries deliberately targeted and recheck commencement orders, regulator rules and factual thresholds.
